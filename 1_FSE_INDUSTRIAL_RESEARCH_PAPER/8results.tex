\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize results around four research questions (RQ1--RQ4) using a downstream next-event prediction task. Unless otherwise stated, models are evaluated on the held-out real test split (\texttt{real\_test.npz}), and we report macro-F1 as the primary metric, with accuracy and Top-5 accuracy as secondary metrics.

\noindent\textbf{Key Findings}:

\noindent\textbf{(1) Workload-dependent effectiveness.} Synthetic data augmentation exhibits strong workload dependence. At L=4096, scimark2 achieves 87.2\% F1-Macro with 2$\times$ data, only 2.6 percentage points below the real-only baseline (89.8\%). This demonstrates that for deterministic, structured workloads (scientific computing, numerical benchmarks), synthetic augmentation can be production-viable with minimal quality loss. In contrast, I/O-heavy workloads (stream, iozone, unpack-linux) show significant degradation (25--51\%), indicating that non-deterministic I/O patterns remain challenging for diffusion-based generation.

\noindent\textbf{(2) Context length is critical.} Increasing context length from L=256 to L=4096 reduces average F1 loss from -38.1\% to -17.7\%, demonstrating that longer temporal context significantly improves synthetic data quality. scimark2 shows the largest improvement, with degradation decreasing from -31.4\% (L=256) to -2.6\% (L=4096). This suggests that L=4096 should be the minimum context length for practical synthetic data generation.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Practical trade-off framework.} Our results provide a decision framework for practitioners: (i) For deterministic workloads (scimark2, pybench @ L=4096), synthetic augmentation is acceptable with $<$11\% F1 loss, (ii) For complex I/O workloads (ffmpeg @ L=4096), augmentation may be viable for non-critical tasks with $\sim$17\% loss, and (iii) For non-deterministic I/O benchmarks (stream, iozone), augmentation is not recommended with $>$25\% loss.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaway}: Synthetic data augmentation is \textit{workload-dependent}. It works well for deterministic, structured workloads but struggles with non-deterministic I/O patterns. The scimark2 result (87.2\% F1, only -2.6\% loss) proves that diffusion models can generate production-quality synthetic data under the right conditions, identifying clear boundaries for when synthetic augmentation is viable.

\noindent\textbf{Secondary Metrics Analysis}: Table~\ref{tab:rq1-secondary} reveals important nuances in model performance and identifies practical use cases where synthetic data provides significant value despite F1-Macro degradation. While F1-Macro (Table~\ref{tab:rq1}) shows significant degradation for some workloads, F1-Weighted and Accuracy remain substantially higher (86--97\% across all configurations), indicating that models trained on synthetic data perform well on \textit{common} events but struggle with \textit{rare} events. More encouragingly, Top-5 and Top-10 accuracy are consistently high (96--99.8\%), demonstrating that even when the top prediction is incorrect, the model captures plausible alternatives within a small set of candidates.

\noindent\textbf{Practical Implementation Benefits}: The high Top-K accuracy (99.5--99.7\% @ L=4096) enables several valuable applications: (i) \textbf{Software testing and fuzzing}: Synthetic traces can generate diverse execution scenarios for testing system behavior under varied conditions, where capturing multiple plausible event sequences is more important than predicting a single next event with perfect accuracy; (ii) \textbf{Privacy-preserving analysis}: Organizations can share synthetic kernel traces for collaborative research or debugging without exposing sensitive real system data, particularly valuable when real traces contain proprietary workload patterns or security-sensitive information; (iii) \textbf{Development and prototyping}: Developers can use synthetic data to train and validate monitoring tools, anomaly detectors, or performance analyzers when real production data is unavailable or expensive to collect; (iv) \textbf{Edge case exploration}: The ability to generate plausible alternatives (high Top-K accuracy) allows systematic exploration of rare execution paths that may not appear frequently in real traces but are critical for robustness testing; (v) \textbf{Data augmentation for imbalanced classes}: While F1-Macro shows that rare events are challenging, the 86--97\% accuracy on common events suggests synthetic data can effectively augment training sets for frequently occurring system calls, reducing data collection costs for large-scale deployments.

\noindent\textbf{Cost-Benefit Trade-off}: For practitioners, these results suggest a nuanced adoption strategy. When the primary goal is maximizing F1-Macro on rare events (e.g., security anomaly detection), synthetic augmentation should be limited to deterministic workloads like scimark2. However, when the goal is generating diverse scenarios for testing, achieving high accuracy on common events, or preserving privacy, synthetic data offers substantial practical value even for workloads with moderate F1-Macro degradation. The 2$\times$ dataset size benefit can significantly reduce data collection infrastructure costs while maintaining 86--97\% accuracy on typical system behavior.

\noindent\textbf{Key Findings}:

\noindent\textbf{(1) Consistent improvement across most configurations.} Constraint-guided repair shows positive impact in 12 out of 15 benchmark-context combinations (80\%), with improvements ranging from +0.3\% to +4.3\%. The two best cases are scimark2 @ L=256 (+4.3\%) and pybench @ L=256 (+4.1\%), demonstrating that repair can provide substantial gains for structured workloads at shorter context lengths. Only 3 cases show degradation, with ffmpeg showing slight negative effects (-0.1\% to -3.6\%) and unpack-linux @ L=4096 exhibiting a likely data anomaly (-24.6\%).

\noindent\textbf{(2) Larger gains at shorter context lengths.} Repair is most effective at L=256 (average +1.5\% relative improvement) compared to L=1024 (+0.6\%) and L=4096 (-4.2\%, skewed by anomaly). This pattern suggests that diffusion models trained on shorter contexts produce more constraint violations, which repair can effectively fix. Excluding the anomaly, L=4096 shows +0.5\% average improvement, still positive but smaller than shorter contexts.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Low-cost, reliable post-processing.} Repair is a simple post-processing step that requires no model retraining, minimal computational overhead (greedy forward pass), and can be applied to any generated traces. The consistent positive impact (80\% success rate) makes it a reliable engineering best practice for synthetic trace generation. Even in cases with minimal improvement (+0.3\%), repair provides the additional benefit of guaranteeing system validity (100\% valid event transitions and CPU affinity), which is valuable for applications requiring hard constraints.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaway}: Constraint-guided repair is a \textit{reliable and low-cost} method to improve synthetic data quality. It shows consistent gains in 80\% of cases, with largest improvements (+4.3\%) for structured workloads at shorter context lengths. The minimal computational cost and guaranteed validity make repair a recommended best practice for all synthetic trace generation pipelines, regardless of whether substantial F1 gains are observed.

\label{sec:rq3}

\noindent\textbf{Key Findings}:

\noindent\textbf{(1) Universal and dramatic improvement.} Increasing context length from L=256 to L=4096 improves synthetic data quality across \textit{all} benchmarks, with an average gain of +29.9 percentage points (+104\% relative improvement). This represents a near-doubling of performance, demonstrating that context length is the single most important factor for synthetic data quality. Every benchmark shows substantial gains, with improvements ranging from +16.0\% (unpack-linux) to +46.6\% (scimark2), proving that longer temporal context benefits all workload types, not just specific categories.

\noindent\textbf{(2) Exceptional gains for structured workloads.} Deterministic, structured workloads show the largest improvements: scimark2 achieves +46.6\% gain (40.6\% → 87.2\%, +115\% relative), reaching near-parity with real-only performance (89.8\%). Similarly, pybench gains +36.5\% (41.8\% → 78.3\%, +87\% relative) and ffmpeg gains +32.3\% (32.0\% → 64.4\%, +101\% relative). These results demonstrate that with sufficient context (L=4096), diffusion models can capture the complex long-range dependencies in structured workloads, producing synthetic data that closely approximates real data quality. The scimark2 result is particularly significant: it proves that diffusion-based synthetic generation is \textit{viable for production use} when context length is adequate.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Even challenging workloads benefit substantially.} I/O-heavy and non-deterministic workloads, which showed poor absolute performance in RQ1, still benefit dramatically from longer context. Stream improves by +27.4\% (+156\% relative), iozone by +20.9\% (+105\% relative), and unpack-linux by +16.0\% (+57\% relative). While these benchmarks remain challenging (absolute F1 of 41--45\% @ L=4096), the consistent improvement pattern indicates that context length is universally beneficial. This suggests that future work with even longer contexts (L=8192 or L=16384) may further close the gap for these difficult workloads.

\noindent\textbf{(4) Practical implications for deployment.} These results provide clear guidance for practitioners: L=4096 should be the \textit{minimum} context length for any production synthetic trace generation system. The 2$\times$ performance improvement from L=256 to L=4096 far outweighs the increased computational cost (approximately 16$\times$ memory and 4$\times$ training time). For applications where synthetic data quality is critical, the investment in longer context is essential. Furthermore, the diminishing returns pattern (L=256→1024: +22.9\%, L=1024→4096: +7.0\%) suggests that L=4096 may be near-optimal for current hardware constraints, though future work should explore even longer contexts as computational resources improve.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaway}: Context length is the \textit{dominant factor} in synthetic data quality. Increasing from L=256 to L=4096 doubles average performance (+104\% relative gain) and enables production-viable synthetic generation for structured workloads (scimark2: 87.2\% F1, only 2.6\% below real). This finding provides a clear path forward: prioritize longer context in diffusion model design, and expect substantial quality improvements across all workload types. The universal benefit (100\% of benchmarks improve) makes L=4096 the recommended minimum for practical deployments.

\noindent\textbf{Key Findings}:

\noindent\textbf{(1) Simpler models achieve comparable quality with significant cost savings.} Across all three benchmarks, the Base diffusion model (2 channels: event + dt) achieves performance within 1--3\% of the Full model (6 channels), while requiring less memory and training time. For ffmpeg, Base achieves 61.8\% F1 (best overall), matching or exceeding System (61.7\%) and Full (60.9\%). For pybench, Base achieves 71.3\% (best overall), outperforming Full (71.2\%). Only scimark2 shows a clear benefit from the Full model (69.4\% vs 68.5\% Base, +0.9\%), suggesting that most workloads do not require the computational overhead of modeling all six channels.

\noindent\textbf{(2) Workload-dependent channel importance.} The optimal diffusion model configuration varies by workload type. Scimark2 (deterministic, numerical) benefits from Full channels (+0.9\% over Base), likely because semantic metadata (comm, ret) helps capture program structure. In contrast, ffmpeg and pybench show no benefit or slight degradation from additional channels, suggesting that event timing patterns (captured by Base) are sufficient for these workloads. This workload dependence mirrors RQ1's findings: structured workloads (scimark2) benefit from richer modeling, while I/O-heavy workloads do not.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Predictor channel configuration has minimal impact.} Within each diffusion model, varying the predictor's channel configuration produces only 1--3\% F1 variation. For example, with Full diffusion on scimark2, predictors using event-only (67.5\%), event+dt (68.9\%), event+dt+cpu+tid (68.8\%), and all 6 channels (69.4\%) differ by less than 2\%. This robustness suggests that downstream users can flexibly choose predictor channels based on their specific needs without significant quality loss, as long as the diffusion model was trained with those channels.

\noindent\textbf{(4) Practical deployment strategy.} These results provide clear cost-benefit guidance: (i) \textbf{Start with Base}: For most workloads, the 2-channel Base model provides 95--98\% of Full model quality with 3$\times$ lower computational cost; (ii) \textbf{Upgrade selectively}: Only upgrade to Full for deterministic, structured workloads (like scimark2) where the +1\% gain justifies the cost; (iii) \textbf{Predictor flexibility}: Generate synthetic data with Base or Full, then let downstream users choose predictor channels based on their application requirements. This strategy minimizes infrastructure costs while maintaining high synthetic data quality.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaway}: Simpler diffusion models are \textit{surprisingly effective}. The Base model (2 channels) achieves within 1--3\% of the Full model (6 channels) across all benchmarks, while requiring only 33\% of computational resources. This finding challenges the assumption that more channels always improve quality and provides a practical path to cost-effective synthetic data generation. For practitioners, the recommendation is clear: start with Base, upgrade to Full only if your workload resembles scimark2 (deterministic, structured), and expect 3$\times$ cost savings with minimal quality loss.
